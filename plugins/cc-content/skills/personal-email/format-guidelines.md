# Personal Email Format Guidelines

_Last updated: 2026-11-11_

These apply to every personal email: a message written by one identifiable person to one recipient or a small set of named recipients, to reach a concrete outcome while protecting the relationship and the sender's reputation. This covers professional one-to-one email (networking, asks, career moves, stakeholder and status email, saying no) as well as private email (invitations, thanks, condolences, group plans). It does not cover bulk campaigns, newsletters, nurture sequences or sales outreach — those belong to `marketing-email`.

The standard marketing axes do not fit this format, so the three layers are mapped to the axes the research actually measures:

| Template axis      | Used here instead                                                                |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| B2B vs. B2C        | Relationship and context: tie strength, power direction, setting, culture, law   |
| Six content goals  | Nine email families (networking, asks, career, visibility, stakeholder, …)       |
| Funnel stage       | Position in the exchange: first contact, reply, follow-up, reconnect, close loop |
| Audience expertise | Recipient familiarity: with the sender and with the topic                        |

All word counts are **English-equivalent units** — translate proportionally for other languages (German typically +10–20%).

**Evidence labels.** Strongest evidence: length and number of asks (field experiments), tone misperception, gratitude and reaching out, trust costs of perceived AI authorship. Subject-line conventions, BLUF, follow-up cadence, signatures, and most cultural and German etiquette guidance are practitioner consensus. Usage data (Boomerang, Hunter and similar) is correlational and vendor-sourced: use it for direction, not precise targets.

## Layer 1 — Universal Best Practices

These apply to every personal email regardless of relationship, goal, or position in the exchange.

### Length and scope

| Context                                | Range                                        | Notes                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First contact or request               | 50–125 words                                 | Randomised field experiment: cutting a request from 127 to 49 words raised response from 2.7% to 4.8% (Rogers & Lasky-Fink, 2023). Boomerang (40M+ warm emails): every length from 50–125 words had response rates above 50% |
| Cold first contact, executives         | 50–100 words                                 | The colder the relationship, the shorter the email                                                                                                                                                                           |
| Reply                                  | Match the other person's length and register | Correspondents converge in length and timing early in threads (Kooti et al., 2015)                                                                                                                                           |
| Relationship upkeep (thanks, congrats) | 30–100 words                                 | Specificity matters more than length                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| Report, brief, handover                | Content-driven                               | Put the decision or ask first; use headings; push detail into an attachment                                                                                                                                                  |
| Paragraphs                             | 1–3 sentences                                | One idea per paragraph                                                                                                                                                                                                       |

The data show a plateau, not a magic number. Below ~50 words a stranger may lack the context to say yes; above ~150 the ask gets buried. Length is a proxy for what matters: how many ideas and requests the email carries and how visible the ask is. Practical rule: draft, then cut by about 30%.

### Required sections

1. **Subject line** — triage and later search: a specific summary or action label, key words first
2. **Greeting** — sets the register and shows the email isn't mail-merged: correct name, title and formality for the relationship and culture (see Layer 2)
3. **Opening line** — doubles as preview text: the purpose or ask, plus one line of context only if needed. For strangers: who you are and why _them_
4. **Body** — only what the recipient needs to say yes or act; one idea per paragraph, ordered by priority
5. **The ask** — the single action wanted: specific, low-effort, with a deadline and its real reason (or deliberately no ask, see below)
6. **Closing** — lowers pressure and adds warmth: explicit freedom to decline, thanks
7. **Sign-off and signature** — relationship marker and identity: consistent, register-appropriate, minimal

**Signature design.** Include full name, role and organisation (or a one-line descriptor) and one primary link or contact route. Optional: pronouns, phone, time zone. Avoid quotes, more than 2–3 links, large images, corporate disclaimers on private email.

### Hook / opening

Use **BLUF (bottom line up front)** — the inverted pyramid applied to email: the ask or conclusion first, then the minimum context, then optional detail. The label comes from military and government style guides and is untested as such; the mechanism is tested — busy readers skim, so the key information must be immediately visible (Rogers & Lasky-Fink, 2023).

- "I hope this email finds you well" or "My name is…" wastes the preview slot.
- For strangers and weak ties, the shared context or mutual connection goes in line 1 ("We met at X — I asked about Y").
- **Exception:** condolences, apologies and relationship emails lead with the emotional message, not a request.

> **Before (146 words, ask in paragraph 3):** "I hope this finds you well. I've been following your work for a while… so I was wondering whether you might have time for a call?"
> **After (55 words, ask first):** "Could I ask you three questions about moving from research into product management? A 20-minute call in the next two weeks would be ideal. I'm a UX researcher at a logistics firm; your talk on mixed-methods roadmaps is why I'm asking you specifically."

### Call to action (the ask)

- **One email, one ask.** Each additional request lowers the odds that any is done ("the more actions a message asks of readers, the less likely readers are to do any one of them" — Rogers & Lasky-Fink). If several answers are needed, number the questions so each can be answered on its own, or send separate emails.
- **Some emails deliberately have no ask.** Thanks, congratulations, condolences and no-strings sharing lose their value when an ask is attached. Say so where it helps: "No need to reply."
- **Closed questions and concrete options:** "Tuesday 10:00 or Thursday 14:00 (CET)?" beats "When are you free?"
- **Deadline plus its real reason:** "Could you let me know by Thursday? I confirm the room Friday." Adding a reason raised compliance from 60% to 93–94% for small requests (Langer et al., 1978); for larger requests only a real reason helped, and a partial replication failed — so give the genuine reason, never a filler one.
- **Size the ask to the relationship:** one question or 15 minutes from a stranger; a full review from a close colleague.
- **Make replying effortless:** "Yes/no plus one sentence is plenty", or draft the text they would otherwise have to write (a forwardable intro blurb, a testimonial draft they can edit).
- **Say when it is not urgent.** Receivers overestimate how fast senders expect replies, especially outside working hours (Giurge & Bohns, 2021, eight preregistered experiments). "No reply needed before Monday" removes that pressure.
- **High-stakes asks to strangers:** people asked face to face were 34 times more likely to comply than people asked by email, while requesters expected no difference (Roghanizad & Bohns, 2017). Use email to open the door to a call rather than to make the whole ask.

### Formatting conventions

- Paragraphs of 1–3 sentences; blank line between them
- Bullets for three or more parallel items; **numbered questions** so the reply can say "1: yes, 2: Tuesday"
- **Bold** at most one element — usually the ask or deadline
- The ask must be visible within the first ~3 lines on a phone
- Plain text: no coloured fonts, images or heavy HTML — they read as marketing
- Self-contained: the core fact lives in the body ("The budget is €48k, up €4k; details attached", not "See attached"). Attach what must be kept or forwarded; link living documents after checking access. Unexpected links from strangers look like phishing
- **Forwardable:** full names, one-line context, nothing disparaging, no in-jokes — introductions, referrals and escalations get forwarded
- Subject lines: a summary or action label ("Decision needed by Fri 14 Nov: offsite venue", "Intro: Dana ↔ Lee (grid analytics)"); 3–7 words; front-load key words because mobile truncates at ~33–50 characters. Bracketed prefixes ([Action], [Decision], [FYI]) suit teams that already use them; with strangers they look bureaucratic. EOM subjects ("Dinner moved to 7 pm (EOM)") suit simple logistics with people who know the convention
- **To/CC/BCC are social signals:** To = expected to act; CC = needs to know, say so ("Copying Sam for visibility, no action needed"); BCC for private group emails to people who don't know each other and to move an introducer out of a thread — announced ("Moving Alex to BCC, thanks!"). Never secretly BCC someone's manager; CCing a boss is an escalation and must be open. Reply-all only if every recipient needs the reply

### Tone in text-only communication

- **Senders overestimate how clearly tone comes across.** Senders predicted 78% of their email statements would be read correctly; 56% were (Kruger et al., 2005). Byron (2008) theorises that neutral work email reads as negative and positive as neutral, stronger for higher-status senders and short messages.
- **The evidence is mixed.** A 2025 study found senders' and receivers' tone ratings aligned well in established relationships (Pollmann & Roos, 2025). Synthesis: strangers and short, context-poor messages need explicit tone signals; close contacts need far fewer.
- **Derived rule:** add one explicit warmth or intent sentence to every non-routine email, and re-read anything critical as the most anxious plausible recipient would.
- **Emoji:** in work email to strangers, smileys did not raise warmth but lowered perceived competence (Glikson et al., 2018). Use them only where the relationship already supports an informal register.
- **Exclamation marks:** one in a greeting or thanks reads as warmth; several read as anxious or fake.
- **Hedging:** one softener before a request upward is courteous; stacked hedges hide the ask. Don't over-apologise — it raises the recipient's sense of imposition.

> **Before:** "Need the numbers by EOD." → **After:** "Could you send the Q3 numbers by 5 pm? I'm presenting them tomorrow morning. Thanks for pulling this together on short notice."

### Authenticity and AI assistance

This skill drafts with AI, so this section governs every run.

- **Suspicion alone lowers trust.** People suspected of using AI smart replies were rated less cooperative and less affiliative, whether or not they used AI (Hohenstein et al., 2023). Disclosed AI involvement lowered trust in email senders, most strongly in condolence contexts; specific personal details helped preserve perceived care (Liu, Mittal, Yang & Bruckman, 2022).
- **The trigger is perceived AI authorship:** generic, over-polished, uniformly positive phrasing, stacked enthusiasm, triads of adjectives, generic praise, stiff transitions.
- **Templated structure, bespoke content.** A reliable skeleton is fine; the sentences must not be sendable unchanged to someone else. **Test:** could this email go unchanged to another recipient? If yes, it will read as automated.
- **The human fingerprint comes from the sender:** the ask, the reason, and at least one detail only the sender would know. AI may shorten, restructure and check tone; it must never supply feelings, memories, compliments or facts about the recipient.
- **Condolences, apologies, deeply personal messages:** build around the sender's own words; never invent the emotional core.

### What separates high from low performers

**Answered emails** put the ask in the first two lines; carry one concrete, appropriately sized ask with a real reason and deadline; say why _this_ recipient; include a warmth signal and freedom to decline; can be answered in under two minutes; make the sender's identity clear in one line.

**Ignored emails** bury the ask, bundle vague requests ("thoughts?"), manufacture urgency, could have been sent to anyone, or need research to answer.

**Reputation compounds.** Email is a repeated game: every message samples the sender's reliability, clarity and warmth. Acknowledge within one business day ("Got it — full reply by Thursday"), meet the deadlines you set, close loops, keep one default sign-off per register (a sudden change reads as a relational signal), and keep name, role and voice consistent with your profiles and website.

## Layer 2 — Relationship and Context Variations

This layer replaces the B2B/B2C split. Five axes shift the Layer 1 defaults. The common mechanisms: shared knowledge reduces uncertainty, status amplifies perceived negativity, and correspondents converge in style early in a thread.

### Relationship and setting

| Dimension            | Stranger (cold)                                                                                    | Weak or dormant tie                                            | Warm contact (referred, existing working relationship)           | Close relationship                                   |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Length               | Shortest: 50–100 words                                                                             | 75–125 words                                                   | 75–150 words                                                     | Whatever reads natural; 30–100 for most              |
| Tone                 | Most formal for the culture; explicit warmth sentence                                              | Semi-formal; name the shared context first                     | Collegial; relational texture expected                           | Write as you speak; over-formality reads as distance |
| Credibility / proof  | One-line identity; why _them_; named mutual connection; accurate, specific reference to their work | The shared-history detail itself                               | The referrer's name does part of the work                        | None needed; over-explaining reads as distance       |
| Recipient psychology | Processes the request with suspicion; can ignore at zero cost                                      | Remembers the tie only if reminded; otherwise effectively cold | Trust transfers from the referrer                                | Expects authenticity, not technique                  |
| Ask size             | One question or 15 minutes; easy out                                                               | Light; or wait until they reply                                | Can be slightly larger; keep it small enough to honour the intro | Larger asks tolerated; still give an easy out        |
| Reading context      | Filtered inbox, triage mode                                                                        | Triage mode                                                    | Normal attention                                                 | Personal attention                                   |

- **Dormant ties are valuable** — executives rated advice from reconnected dormant ties as comparably or more useful than from current ties (Levin, Walter & Murnighan, 2011) — but reconnection fails when the tie isn't refreshed before the ask (Rondi, Levin & De Massis, 2023). People also underestimate how much others appreciate being reached out to, especially weak ties (Liu, Rim, Min & Min, 2022).
- **Power direction.** _Upward:_ shorter, BLUF even earlier, lead with a recommendation and a binary choice ("I recommend option B; can you approve by Friday?"), no open-ended "thoughts?". _Peers:_ direct and collaborative. _Downward:_ a terse note from a senior person is easily read as displeasure (Byron, 2008) — add context and appreciation, explain the reason, preserve the recipient's ability to say no or propose an alternative, say "no rush" when true.
- **Internal vs. external.** Internal: shared context and conventions (EOM, [Action]) help; jargon makes the email non-forwardable. External: self-contained, acronyms expanded, write for whoever it may be forwarded to.
- **Professional vs. private.** Professional email leads with purpose; private email leads with warmth. Register follows the relationship, not the platform. In the blurry middle (colleague-friends, alumni, clients who became friends) match the register the recipient uses with you, err one notch more formal than their last message, and flag a topic switch ("Separate, personal question: …").

### Culture

These frameworks describe averages, not individuals — treat them as hypotheses.

- **Indirect, relationship-first cultures:** one relational sentence before the ask; read soft replies ("this may be difficult") as a possible no.
- **Low-context, direct cultures:** BLUF and explicit deadlines are valued; heavy hedging reads as evasive.
- **Hierarchical cultures:** keep titles until invited to drop them; ask the introducer about routing norms.
- **Flexible-time cultures:** give the reason for a deadline and confirm it explicitly.

### German-speaking countries (DACH)

Practitioner consensus (Knigge trainers, DIN 5008, university guidance) plus one court ruling.

**Sie vs. Du**

- Use **Sie** for first professional contact unless the sector or organisation has a declared Du culture (many start-ups, tech, creative, outdoor/sports). If the recipient's website or signature uses Du, mirroring is usually safe.
- Traditionally the more senior, older or host party offers the switch. Switch only after an explicit offer ("Wollen wir uns duzen?") or after the other person starts using Du. **Never switch unilaterally.** Reverting from Du to Sie signals a rupture.
- "Hamburger Sie" (first name + Sie) is a recognised middle path; surname + Du is inconsistent.

**Salutations**

| Salutation                                               | Register               | When it fits                                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sehr geehrte Frau Dr. Weber, / Sehr geehrter Herr Weber, | Most formal; DIN 5008  | First contact, applications, authorities, senior recipients                           |
| Guten Tag Frau Weber, / Guten Tag Alex Weber,            | Neutral-formal, modern | First contact where "Sehr geehrte" feels stiff; first name + surname avoids gendering |
| Liebe Frau Weber, / Lieber Herr Weber,                   | Warm-formal (Sie)      | Once a working relationship exists                                                    |
| Hallo Frau Weber,                                        | Informal-polite        | Ongoing or internal contact; too casual for some first contacts                       |
| Liebe Alex, / Hallo Alex,                                | Du                     | Du relationships, private life                                                        |
| Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren,                           | Formal, anonymous      | Only if no name can be found; alternatives "Guten Tag," / "Sehr geehrtes Team von …"  |

**Titles:** in Germany and Austria include academic titles ("Sehr geehrte Frau Dr. Weber"); with professors use only the highest title ("Sehr geehrter Herr Professor Meier"). Austria uses titles more widely (Mag., DI); Switzerland generally less. "Hallo Prof" without a surname is a faux pas.

**Sign-offs**

| Sign-off                                                  | Register                       |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Mit freundlichen Grüßen                                   | Formal default; distanced      |
| Freundliche Grüße / Beste Grüße                           | Modern professional            |
| Viele Grüße                                               | Friendly professional          |
| Herzliche Grüße                                           | Warm, established relationship |
| Liebe Grüße                                               | Personal; not for business     |
| MfG, LG, VG                                               | Avoid professionally           |
| Freundliche Grüsse (CH, no ß); Schöne Grüße aus Wien (AT) | Regional markers               |

Some trainers flag "Danke im Voraus" as presumptuous.

**Gendered salutations.** OLG Frankfurt am Main (21 June 2022, 9 U 92/20) held that forcing a non-binary customer to choose "Herr" or "Frau" violated the AGG. For individuals use their known preference, otherwise "Guten Tag [first name] [surname]"; for groups "Liebes Team" or "Guten Tag zusammen". The gender asterisk is contested in some organisations.

**Directness:** German professional email is typically more direct and task-focused than British or American email; vagueness reads as evasive rather than polite. Swiss email is often perceived as more indirect, Austrian as more title-conscious.

**German speakers writing in English:** English removes Sie/Du — start with "Dear Dr Weber" for senior recipients and mirror. Literal translations can sound demanding ("I expect your answer until Friday" → "Could you let me know by Friday?"). Add one warmth sentence, because German directness plus email's negativity effect can read as curt. "Kind regards" is the neutral equivalent of "Mit freundlichen Grüßen"; "Best regards" is slightly lighter.

### Legal boundaries for unsolicited one-to-one email (EU/DACH)

**Not legal advice; status as of November 2026 — laws and case law change, so verify before relying on it.** The core distinction: a genuinely personal or networking message (asking for advice, an informational interview, an introduction; an application; a thank-you) is generally _not_ advertising. A message promoting the sender's or employer's goods or services generally _is_, even when written by hand to one person.

- **Germany:** § 7 Abs. 2 Nr. 2 UWG treats email advertising without _prior express consent_ as an unreasonable nuisance, toward businesses as well as consumers. A single email can be enough (BGH, I ZR 218/07). A published contact address is not consent (BGH, I ZR 201/07); a LinkedIn connection is not consent either (AG Düsseldorf, Nov 2025). "Advertising" is read broadly — a satisfaction survey attached to an invoice email counted (BGH, VI ZR 225/17).
- **Austria:** § 174 Abs. 3 TKG 2021 bans email for direct marketing without prior consent, with no business/consumer distinction. The existing-customer exception in § 174 Abs. 4 is narrow: being a customer is not enough — it requires, among other conditions, that the address was obtained in connection with a sale, that the email markets the sender's own similar products or services, and that the recipient could opt out at collection and in every message. Verify the conditions before relying on it.
- **Switzerland:** UWG Art. 3 Abs. 1 lit. o targets _mass_ advertising; individually sent email is mostly outside it, but a single researched 1:1 commercial email is disputed.
- **GDPR** applies on top: an email address is personal data. A legitimate interest does not override the UWG/TKG consent rules.

| Message type                                                    | Typical classification (DE/AT)                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Advice, informational-interview or intro request with no offer  | Generally not advertising                                                      |
| Job application; thanks; condolences                            | Not advertising                                                                |
| "Networking" email ending with an offer of your paid services   | Likely advertising → needs consent                                             |
| Pitching yourself as a paid speaker or freelancer to a stranger | Grey zone leaning advertising; get consent — a warm intro alone is not consent |
| Story pitch to a journalist                                     | Usually press relations, but unsettled; keep it non-promotional                |
| Asking a client for a testimonial                               | Possibly advertising (cf. BGH survey ruling)                                   |

### Persuasion principles

Personal email can be re-read, forwarded and screenshotted. Techniques that rely on momentary face-to-face pressure shrink in text, and any manipulation becomes _documented_. The robust, ethical levers are reasons, specificity, gratitude, small honest asks, and easy exits. Ranked by strength of evidence:

| Rank | Principle                                                | Evidence                                                                                                                                                                      | How to apply in personal email                                                                                                | Failure mode                                                                      |
| ---- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Asking directly; calibrating to the channel              | Strong (Flynn & Lake, 2008; Roghanizad & Bohns, 2017)                                                                                                                         | People underestimate in-person compliance but overestimate email compliance: do ask, and move big asks to a call              | Relying on email alone for big asks to strangers                                  |
| 2    | Gratitude and reaching out                               | Strong, preregistered (Kumar & Epley, 2018; Liu et al., 2022)                                                                                                                 | Unprompted, specific thanks and check-ins build relationship capital cheaply; recipients value them more than senders predict | Thanks that is a disguised ask is resented                                        |
| 3    | Giving real reasons                                      | Moderate (Langer et al., 1978; partial replication failed)                                                                                                                    | The reason is written and re-readable, so it must be specific and true                                                        | "Because I need it" reads as manipulative                                         |
| 4    | Reciprocity — give before asking                         | Moderate (general literature)                                                                                                                                                 | Give something useful _unconditionally_, kept clearly separate from any later ask                                             | A gift immediately followed by an ask feels transactional                         |
| 5    | Similarity and shared identity (Cialdini's liking/unity) | Moderate (general literature)                                                                                                                                                 | Real shared context (alma mater, event, mutual contact) belongs in line 1 for strangers                                       | Trivial or invented similarity ("We both like coffee!") reads as a template       |
| 6    | Autonomy-preserving language ("but you are free")        | Moderate, major caveats: Carpenter (2013) found roughly doubled compliance; Fillon et al. (2023) found g = 0.44 overall but no significant effect in low-risk-of-bias studies | Use as sincere courtesy, matched by a genuinely low-pressure email; expect a small effect                                     | As a formula it reads passive-aggressive ("Feel free to ignore this, of course…") |
| 7    | Foot-in-the-door                                         | Moderate (Freedman & Fraser, 1966)                                                                                                                                            | Legitimate version: start small (one question before a call)                                                                  | An engineered escalation is visible in the written thread                         |
| 8    | Door-in-the-face                                         | Replicated but small; non-significant for behaviour                                                                                                                           | Only as honest scoping ("Ideal is 1 hour; if too much, could 20 minutes work?")                                               | An extreme first ask by email often gets no reply at all                          |
| 9    | Ben Franklin effect                                      | Weak; no large preregistered replication                                                                                                                                      | Genuine small requests for advice may build liking                                                                            | Engineering favours to manipulate liking has no support                           |

**Excluded:** scarcity and loss framing. Manufactured urgency teaches recipients to ignore your urgency, and in a written record it reads as pressure. Social proof and authority work only as plain relevance ("Priya Shah suggested I contact you"; "I've run this survey for three cohorts"), never as credential-dropping.

**Personalisation:** genuine means a reason only this recipient fits; superficial means merge fields and generic praise. Test: could this sentence have been written about anyone else? If yes, it isn't personalisation — and it now signals automation, which lowers trust.

Common mistake across all families: too much background before the request.

**1. Networking and introductions.** Cold outreach: one line on who you are, one specific reason for writing to _them_ (a talk, paper or decision of theirs), a small ask (15–20 minutes or two questions), an easy out; a mutual connection goes in line 1. Asking for an introduction: explain why this person and what you want, include a separate 3–4-sentence forwardable blurb, let the introducer decline gracefully. Making an introduction: **double opt-in** — ask each party privately first; then one new email ("Intro: A ↔ B") with two sentences on each person and why they should talk, and step back ("I'll let you take it from here"). Event follow-up: within 1–3 days, reference the specific conversation and deliver what you promised.

> **Before:** "I'd love to connect and pick your brain sometime!" → **After:** "Your answer at Tuesday's panel on rural broadband grants stuck with me. Could I ask two follow-up questions by email or in a 15-minute call next week? Completely fine if you're swamped."

**2. Asks and favours.** Specify scope ("Is the opening persuasive? Yes/no plus one sentence"), effort and deadline with its reason. Feedback: say which kind (direction, clarity, proofreading) and how far along the work is. Referrals and testimonials: draft text they can edit; ask only people with direct experience of your work (DACH: see the legal table). Research interviews: duration, format, incentive, data use, freedom to decline. People underestimate others' willingness to help (Flynn & Lake, 2008) — so ask.

**3. Career moves.**

| Sub-type                | Length  | Key elements                                                                                                                                              |
| ----------------------- | ------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Informational interview | 50–100  | Say explicitly you are not asking for a job; 15 minutes; two specific questions                                                                           |
| Reference request       | 100–150 | Ask whether they can give a _strong_ reference (an easy out); 2–4 weeks ahead; supply job description, deadline, key points, CV; report the outcome later |
| Application             | 75–150  | Follow the employer's instructions; role, top two qualifications, attachments; DACH: Sie and "Sehr geehrte/r" unless the ad uses Du                       |
| Interview thank-you     | 50–100  | Within 24 hours; one specific point from the conversation; no ask                                                                                         |
| Application follow-up   | 50–80   | Polite check-in, reiterate interest, ask for the timeline                                                                                                 |
| Offer negotiation       | 100–150 | Appreciation; one consolidated counter with reasons; propose a call; no piecemeal haggling                                                                |
| Departure announcement  | 50–100  | Short, warm, forward-looking, no criticism; name the successor for external contacts                                                                      |

**4. Visibility pitches.** Speaker or podcast guest: audience benefit and a proposed title first, then 2–3 takeaways, then proof (past talks, samples), then the ask; respect calls for papers. Journalist pitch: the subject line is the story; the first line says why it matters to _their_ readers now; offer data or access; attach nothing. After a talk: within 48 hours share slides, thank organisers with a specific detail, follow up with attendees by referring to their questions — no ask.

**5. Stakeholder communication.** Name the action type and deadline in the subject ("Approval needed by 20 Nov: revised budget"; "For input: draft brief — comments by Friday"). Number your questions. Briefs: objective, audience, deliverable, constraints, deadline, decision-maker, examples of "good"; ask for confirmation. Meeting recaps: same day, decisions/owners/deadlines/open items ("If I don't hear otherwise by Thursday, I'll treat these as agreed"). Results: headline, meaning, next steps.

**6. Status, decision, handover.** Updates: status word or traffic light, what changed, risks, asks. Decision requests: 2–3 options, your recommendation, the deadline with its reason, the consequence of no decision. Escalations: factual and blame-free; say what you tried and tell the people involved you are escalating. Handovers: current state, open items, contacts, file locations, risks — forwardable and self-contained.

> **Before:** "Quick update on the vendor situation… [four paragraphs] … let me know what you think." → **After:** "**Decision needed by Wed 12 Nov:** extend Vendor A (recommended; €4k, no migration risk) or switch to Vendor B (€3k, 6-week migration)? Details below."

**7. Saying no.** The no goes in the first two sentences; a vague "maybe" and over-apologising both invite negotiation. Offer an alternative only if you mean it. Scope or deadline pushback names the trade-off ("A by Friday or A+B by the 20th — which do you prefer?"). Delegating: outcome, deadline, decision rights, check-in point.

> **Before:** "So sorry, things are crazy, maybe later in the year we could possibly look at it?" → **After:** "Thank you for thinking of me. I have to say no this time, as I'm committed through March. Priya Shah may be a great fit; happy to introduce you."

**8. Relationship upkeep.** Thank-you notes: name the specific act and its effect; recipients value thanks more than senders predict (Kumar & Epley, 2018). Congratulations: specific, brief, no ask — not a networking opportunity. Condolences: acknowledge the loss plainly, add a memory if the sender has one, offer specific help or say "no need to reply"; no platitudes, no cause-of-death questions; some DACH etiquette prefers a card. Participants were especially critical of AI involvement in condolences — build around the sender's own words. Check-ins and sharing: "Saw this and thought of your project; no reply needed." Apologies: name what you did and its impact, say what will change, offer a proportionate repair; never "if you were offended"; for serious matters use email to request a conversation.

**9. Private life.** Invitations: the subject gives event and date; first lines cover what, when, where and the RSVP deadline. Ask about dietary needs lightly and ask for direct replies, not reply-all, to protect health information; BCC large lists of people who don't know each other. Group plans: numbered options or a poll, a decision deadline and a decision rule ("I'll book whichever date most can make by Sunday"). Requests to friends, family or neighbours: warm opener, clear ask with a time window, a real easy out, thanks.

### By position in the exchange

This axis replaces the funnel stage.

|                | First contact                                                 | Reply                                                       | Follow-up after silence                     | Ongoing thread                          | Reconnecting after a gap                           | Closing the loop after a favour                              |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Reader mindset | "Who is this and why me?" — high uncertainty                  | Expects an answer to what they asked                        | Busy or undecided; has not said no          | Shares context; needs the current state | Must be reminded of the tie                        | Wants to know their help mattered                            |
| Opening        | Relevance signal and identity                                 | The answer, in line 1                                       | The ask restated in one sentence            | The current state or decision           | Shared context; gap acknowledged lightly           | The outcome                                                  |
| Ask            | Smallest of all; easy out                                     | Only if needed                                              | Shrinks with each step                      | One per thread                          | Light, or none until they reply                    | None; offer reciprocity concretely                           |
| Length         | 50–100 words                                                  | Mirror theirs                                               | Shorter than the original                   | Summarise every 5–10 messages           | 50–150 words                                       | 50–100 words                                                 |
| Common mistake | Context before the ask; promotional purpose (DACH legal risk) | Answering in a different order than asked; no holding reply | Guilt-trip wording; repeating the same text | Topic drift under an old subject        | Pretending the gap didn't happen; over-apologising | Skipping it entirely — the most-skipped, highest-value email |

**Replies:** answer in the first line; reply by number if they numbered their questions; mirror register and length, erring slightly warmer. If you need time, acknowledge quickly and give a date for the full reply.

**Follow-up cadence** (practitioner consensus; most replies arrive quickly or not at all — Kooti et al., 2015, 16 billion emails: half of replies within 47 minutes):

| Step           | Timing                                         | How it differs                                                                                                              |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Follow-up 1    | 3–5 business days (sooner for a real deadline) | Same thread; one-line nudge with the ask restated; assume good faith                                                        |
| Follow-up 2    | 7–10 business days later                       | Add value or shrink the ask (yes/no version, new information, "would a 10-minute call be easier?")                          |
| Close the loop | 10–14 business days later                      | "I'll assume the timing isn't right and won't follow up further. If it becomes useful later, I'd be glad to hear from you." |

At most two follow-ups to strangers. With colleagues who have a real dependency, switch channel (call, chat, in person). In private life, one gentle RSVP reminder is normal. Sales-sequence cadences of 3–5+ follow-ups do not transfer to personal email.

**Ongoing threads:** keep the subject accurate and update it when the topic shifts; start a new thread when the topic or decision changes, after about 10 messages, or for a double opt-in introduction.

**Closing the loop:** thank within 24 hours, then report the outcome when you know it, even months later ("The intro led to a call with X, and we're now exploring Y — thank you"). Offer reciprocity concretely ("If I can return the favour, e.g. reviewing your research plans, just ask").

## Outdated or Counterproductive Tactics

Practices to avoid, not recommendations.

| Tactic                                                                                                                               | Why it may work short-term                           | Why it damages trust                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fake "Re:"/"Fwd:" subjects                                                                                                           | Exploits the "existing thread = important" heuristic | Discovered within seconds; marks you as a sales-sequence sender; Austria also bans concealing the sender in direct marketing |
| False urgency                                                                                                                        | Triggers fast responses                              | Teaches recipients to ignore your urgency; reads as pressure; associated with phishing                                       |
| Guilt-trip or passive-aggressive follow-ups ("I guess this isn't a priority for you", "Per my last email", repeated "Bumping this!") | Social pressure                                      | Text amplifies negativity; permanent and forwardable; can end the relationship                                               |
| Manipulative "breakup" emails                                                                                                        | Loss aversion                                        | An honest close-the-loop is fine; as a recurring trick it's manipulative                                                     |
| Fake personalisation (merge fields, scraped compliments, flattery openers)                                                           | Superficial relevance                                | Easily spotted; now associated with automation and AI, which lowers trust                                                    |
| Clickbait or "Quick question" subjects for big asks                                                                                  | Curiosity opens                                      | The mismatch with the content breaks trust; violates the subject-as-summary norm                                             |
| Open tracking and read receipts ("I saw you opened my email…")                                                                       | Feels like intelligence                              | Unreliable under privacy features; reads as surveillance; ePrivacy/GDPR questions in the EU                                  |
| Placebic reasons                                                                                                                     | May help with trivial asks                           | Fail for real asks; look thoughtless on re-reading                                                                           |
| Door-in-the-face by email                                                                                                            | Small effect in person                               | The first ask often gets no reply; manipulative in a written record                                                          |
| Long sales-style follow-up sequences (3–5+ touches)                                                                                  | Vendor data from cold sales                          | Reads as chasing; personal email allows two follow-ups, then close the loop                                                  |
| "Networking" as cover for selling                                                                                                    | Gets past "no sales" filters                         | Breaches trust; likely unlawful without consent in DE/AT                                                                     |
| Over-apologising, excessive deference                                                                                                | Seems polite                                         | Raises the sense of imposition; lowers perceived competence; buries the ask                                                  |

Principle: if a tactic depends on the recipient misunderstanding something, it damages the sender's personal brand.

## Quality Checklist

**Purpose and ask**

- [ ] The purpose fits in one sentence and appears, with the ask, in the first two lines (emotional message first for condolences, apologies and relationship emails)
- [ ] Exactly one primary ask, or numbered questions that can be answered independently, or deliberately no ask for relationship upkeep — and the ask matches the confirmed family
- [ ] The ask states what, how much effort, and by when; any deadline carries its real reason; a non-urgent email says so
- [ ] The recipient can answer in under two minutes, or a call is proposed

**Length and format**

- [ ] Length fits the confirmed family, tie strength and position (first contact or request ≤ 125 words unless there is a reason)
- [ ] Paragraphs ≤ 3 sentences; at most one bold element; the ask is visible on a phone without scrolling
- [ ] Self-contained and forwardable: a third party could understand it, and nothing is disparaging

**Subject, greeting, sign-off**

- [ ] The subject summarises the content or action (with deadline if relevant); no fake "Re:"/"Fwd:", no clickbait
- [ ] Name, spelling, title and register (e.g. Sie/Du, "Sehr geehrte"/"Liebe") match the relationship and culture; no unilateral switch to an informal register
- [ ] Sign-off matches the sender's usual one for this register; signature minimal (≤ 3 links)

**Tone**

- [ ] One explicit warmth or intent signal in every non-routine email
- [ ] Re-read as the most anxious plausible recipient would, nothing reads curt, sarcastic or blaming
- [ ] No emoji in first-contact, upward or formal email; at most one exclamation mark
- [ ] No over-apologising or over-flattering; saying no or "not now" is made explicitly easy

**Recipients and threading**

- [ ] For strangers and weak ties: identity, why them, and any mutual connection appear in the opening
- [ ] To/CC/BCC are deliberate and announced where relevant; private group lists use BCC; the subject matches the topic

**Authenticity and ethics**

- [ ] At least one detail could only have been written by this sender to this recipient — supplied by the owner, not invented
- [ ] No invented feelings, memories, compliments or facts; generic AI phrasing removed
- [ ] No false urgency, guilt-tripping, fake personalisation or tracking-based follow-up
- [ ] (EU/DACH) The email does not promote a commercial offer to someone who hasn't consented

**Process**

- [ ] Relationship, family, position in the exchange, topic familiarity and language/register confirmed with the owner
- [ ] Tone consistent with the sender's voice guide (if loaded)
- [ ] Readability pass run (`../_shared/readability-patterns.md`): no clusters of coinages, long subject–verb gaps, dropped signpost words, noun stacks, ad-hoc noun-verbs, or objects acting like people
- [ ] Follow-through planned where an ask exists: follow-up date (3–5 business days), close-the-loop plan, outcome report after a favour
