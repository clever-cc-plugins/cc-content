# LinkedIn Post Format Guidelines

_Last updated: 2026-08-04_

Format-scope rules for the linkedin-post skill. These constraints apply to every
generated post regardless of campaign briefing or brand voice.

This file was refreshed against a 2026 research synthesis (six independent research
runs, Aug 2026) covering LinkedIn's post-truncation mechanics, the platform's 2026
"authenticity" push against AI-generated slop and engagement bait, and the structural
gap between personal-profile and company-page performance. Sections marked
**(contested)** reflect genuine disagreement across sources — apply directionally, not
as a hard number.

Word- and sentence-length guidance in this file is **English-equivalent** — translate
proportionally for other languages (German typically +10–20%, Romance languages roughly
equivalent, CJK languages count characters instead, where ~1.6 CJK characters ≈ 1 English
word). Character limits (e.g., the 3,000-character hard maximum) are platform mechanics
and apply identically regardless of language.

---

## Character Limits

| Constraint          | Limit                  | Notes                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hard maximum        | 3,000 characters       | LinkedIn truncates at 3,000                                                                                                         |
| Recommended target  | 1,300–2,500 characters | Highest median engagement band across multiple large-sample 2026 studies; shorter posts (<400 chars) underperform by roughly 20–25% |
| Company-page target | ≤700–800 characters    | Company pages skew shorter and more scannable — see [Personal Profile vs. Company Page](#personal-profile-vs-company-page)          |

Count characters including spaces and newlines. Aim for the recommended target for
personal-profile posts; use the tighter company-page target for pages. Never pad to
hit a number — match length to what the content actually needs. Posts approaching the
3,000-character ceiling show a slight engagement dip versus the 1,300–2,500 band, so
maxing out rarely helps.

## Post Structure

Every post MUST include three sections in this order:

### 1. Hook (before the fold)

LinkedIn truncates behind a "…see more" link at roughly **140 characters on mobile**
and **~210 on desktop** — mobile is the binding constraint since most engagement
happens there. LinkedIn also counts explicit line breaks toward this budget and caps
the visible area at about three lines, so a blank line inserted early in the hook can
trigger truncation well before the character count runs out.

Rules:

- Open with the strongest claim, question, or tension — never bury the lead
- Keep the hook as tight, unbroken prose within ~140 characters (including the
  opening emoji); avoid inserting a blank line inside the first three lines — save
  line breaks for the body, after the hook has already earned the click
- The hook must be specific and concrete, not a vague curiosity-gap tease — lead with
  a real number, a named outcome, or a precise claim over an abstract "you won't
  believe this" tension
- Avoid openers: "I'm excited to share…", "Proud to announce…", "Just wanted to…",
  "Nobody talks about this…", "I wasn't going to post this, but…", "This changed
  everything ↓", "Agree?", and fake-vulnerability bait-and-switch openers (e.g. "I was
  fired. (Just kidding…)") — these read as formula and are increasingly pattern-matched
  and scrolled past by readers and down-ranked by the algorithm
- Avoid the humblebrag arc ("I just hit 100K followers. Here's what I learned.") and
  the generic "I did X. Here's what I learned." / "Stop doing X. Start doing Y."
  templates — specificity and a genuine stance now outperform formula
- Prefer concrete specifics over vague praise: "We cut deploy time by 40%" beats
  "We've been working hard on improving our process"
- One idea only — no compound sentences in the hook

### 2. Body

Delivers the substance the hook promises.

Rules:

- Short paragraphs: 1–3 lines each, separated by blank lines; aim for at least
  14 paragraphs total across hook, body, and CTA
- Sentence length: 10–19 words per paragraph on average (English-equivalent; longer in
  languages with longer average word length — see note above)
- Readability: target 5th–7th grade level (**English-only** metric, no direct equivalent
  in other languages); for non-English output, apply the same underlying principle —
  short sentences, common everyday words, minimal subordinate clauses
- Use line breaks (not bullet points) as the primary rhythm device; bullets are
  acceptable for lists of 3+ parallel items — use one consistent bullet symbol (•, -,
  or →), keep items to 1–2 lines each, and add a blank line before and after the block
- Maintain forward momentum: each paragraph should earn the next
- Concrete > abstract: show numbers, examples, named outcomes where possible
- Avoid passive voice and hedge phrases ("kind of", "sort of", "a bit")
- For educational or how-to content, structure the core content as a saveable
  framework, checklist, or numbered sequence where it fits naturally — 2026 data
  consistently ranks saves and sustained reading time among the strongest engagement
  signals, well above raw likes

### 3. Call to Action (CTA)

The final line or short paragraph. Every post needs one.

Rules:

- One CTA per post — do not stack multiple asks
- Make it specific and easy to answer — a focused question drives significantly more
  engagement than a vague ask; favor prompts that invite a substantive reply over a
  one-word reaction
- Good examples: "Drop your answer in the comments", "DM me the word GUIDE",
  "What's the biggest misconception about [specific topic]?"
- Avoid vague CTAs: "Let me know what you think", "Share if you agree", "Thoughts?"
- **Never use engagement-bait CTAs** — "Comment YES if you agree", "Like if you agree",
  "Agree? 👇", "Tag someone who needs this", "Repost if you agree". These are explicitly
  named across 2026 platform guidance and independent analyses as patterns LinkedIn's
  ranking system detects and actively suppresses, not just weak copy
- CTA should match the post's purpose: engagement, lead gen, or awareness

## Hashtags

2026 sources no longer agree that hashtags reduce reach — the consensus has shifted
to "no longer a reach multiplier, but mildly useful for discoverability and topic
categorization." The magnitude of any residual lift is **(contested)**, ranging across
sources from roughly neutral to a modest engagement bump. None of the research
measures visible usage rates, only effect-when-used, and practitioner observation is
that hashtags have become far less common on the platform in practice — a feature
can keep a small residual effect while going out of fashion. Treat hashtags as an
optional, low-priority lever, not a required element of every post.

| Rule    | Guideline                                                                    |
| ------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Default | Optional. If used, 3–5 relevant, specific hashtags at the very end, own line |
| Omit    | Freely — omitting hashtags entirely is a fine default, not just an exception |
| Format  | CamelCase for multi-word tags (#ContentMarketing not #contentmarketing)      |
| Avoid   | Stacks of 6+ tags (reads as spam); generic or irrelevant trending tags       |

## Tone Calibrations for LinkedIn

LinkedIn sits between Twitter's informality and a business article's formality.
Calibrate by post purpose:

| Purpose               | Tone target                                                                                       |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Thought leadership    | Confident, direct, first-person singular; share a specific stance                                 |
| Company news / launch | Professional but warm; "we" is fine; avoid corporate-speak                                        |
| Educational / tips    | Practical, peer-to-peer; write like explaining to a smart colleague                               |
| Personal story        | Honest and specific; vulnerability is credible, not sentimental                                   |
| Discussion-oriented   | Conversational; end with a genuine, substantive question — never a manufactured engagement prompt |

Regardless of purpose:

- Use "you" and "I/we" — not "one" or "professionals"
- Contractions are acceptable (don't, we've, it's)
- Avoid jargon unless the target audience uses it themselves
- No exclamation marks except in product launch posts (max 1) — this reflects English
  business-writing norms; adapt punctuation frequency to the target language's own
  professional conventions (e.g., some languages tolerate exclamation marks more
  freely in business contexts)

## Formatting Rules

- **Emojis**: 0–3 per post. The largest 2026 dataset (847k+ posts) found the jump
  from 0 to 1 emoji lifts engagement ~7% and reach ~22%, but returns flatten
  completely beyond that — more emojis do not help and heavy use increasingly reads
  as AI/template output. One opening emoji in the hook line is a strong default; use
  it structurally (an attention opener, a single consistent list marker, one CTA
  arrow) rather than decoratively; never stack emojis as bullet substitutes in
  professional/B2B contexts; omit entirely if brand voice prohibits them
- **Bold / italic**: Not natively supported in LinkedIn posts — never fake bold with
  Unicode lookalikes. Beyond looking artificial, Unicode "bold" text is read
  character-by-character or skipped by screen readers, and isn't indexed by
  LinkedIn's search — it degrades accessibility and discoverability for no real gain
- **Links**: Put the link in the post body when it adds real value — that's where it
  belongs for the reader, and LinkedIn's own product team has denied applying an
  automatic reach penalty for in-body links (statements attributed to Rishi
  Jobanputra, reiterated in a March 2026 briefing). **(contested)**: independent
  analyses report reach reductions ranging anywhere from ~19% to ~60% for in-body
  links, so the effect may be real even without an "official" penalty — but do NOT
  default to the "link in first comment" workaround as a way around it. Several 2026
  sources report the workaround itself is now detected and penalized the same way,
  which makes it a manipulative dead end that annoys readers without a reliable
  reach payoff. If a link serves the post, put it in the post; don't write around
  the algorithm
- **@Mentions**: Tag only people or companies genuinely relevant to the post's
  content, and only when they're likely to engage; cap at roughly 3–5 mentions —
  tagging names just to extend reach reads as mention-baiting and risks looking like
  spam
- **Line spacing**: Blank line between every paragraph (but see the Hook section —
  avoid blank lines within the first three lines, before the fold)

## Punctuation and Rhetoric

The rules below describe English typographic and rhetorical conventions. Adapt them to
the target language's own punctuation and rhetorical norms rather than translating them
literally (e.g., dash usage and quotation conventions vary by language). These rules
also double as anti-AI-slop signals — see below.

- **No trailing em-dashes.** Do not use an em-dash to attach a trailing clause,
  adverbial, or list to the preceding sentence. Use a comma, colon, or a new
  sentence instead. Em-dashes are reserved for true parenthetical inserts and
  must always appear in pairs.
  - ✅ "Many teams underestimate the time for code reviews, until the release
    approaches."
  - ✅ Paired insert: "Our team — twelve engineers across three time zones — took
    three months."
  - ❌ "Many teams underestimate the time for code reviews — until the release
    approaches."
- **No rhetorical antitheses as a stylistic device.** Avoid "Not X, but Y" and
  "X, not Y" patterns used purely for a punchy effect. Only contrast two things
  when they genuinely represent distinct alternatives.
  - ✅ "Good code reviews take time, attention, and a clear commitment to quality."
  - ❌ "Good code reviews aren't a control ritual — they're real teamwork."

## Avoiding AI-Slop Signals

LinkedIn's 2026 "authenticity" push (a user-facing "Seems like AI slop" report button
shipped July 2026) targets generic, voiceless content — the mechanism is largely
behavioral: formulaic text earns less dwell time, fewer saves, and fewer substantive
comments, and reach suffers as a result. Using AI to draft or tighten a post is not
the problem; publishing generic output with no specific insight is. Concretely:

- **Never use these clichés**: "in today's fast-paced [industry/digital] landscape",
  "unlock the power of", "delve into", "robust", "synergy", "game-changer", "I'm
  humbled to share", "it's not just X, it's Y" (see the antithesis rule above)
- **Avoid uniform sentence rhythm.** Vary sentence length; an unbroken run of
  same-length sentences reads as machine-generated
- **Every claim needs a specific anchor** — a real number, a named example, a
  concrete outcome. Generic advice with no anchoring detail is the single strongest
  AI-slop tell, independent of phrasing
- **Keep one distinctive voice choice per post** where brand voice allows it (a
  specific word the author actually uses, a recognizable opinion) — differentiation,
  not polish, is what reads as human in 2026

## Personal Profile vs. Company Page

This is the single biggest structural variable in how the copy should be written.
Every large-sample 2026 study reviewed agrees on the direction, even where exact
multipliers differ: **personal-profile posts substantially outperform company-page
posts** — cited figures range roughly 5–10x on reach and engagement, with company
pages organically reaching on the order of 1–2% of their followers versus a
high-single/low-double-digit percentage for personal profiles. Determine which one
this post is for (see SKILL.md Step 3a) and calibrate accordingly:

| Dimension           | Personal profile                                                                  | Company page                                                               |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Voice / POV         | First-person "I" / "we" — opinion, lived experience                               | Brand "we" — factual, on-brand, less "diary"                               |
| Content type        | Thought leadership, commentary, contrarian takes, behind-the-scenes, lessons      | Product news, hiring, partnerships, customer wins, official announcements  |
| Length              | Full recommended target, 1,300–2,500 characters; supports longer narrative arcs   | Tighter, ≤700–800 characters for announcements; scannable over narrative   |
| Vulnerability/humor | Effective when genuine and tied to a professional insight                         | Limited use; indirect at most                                              |
| CTA                 | Open, peer-to-peer discussion questions (peers comment on peers far more readily) | Clear business next step (register, download, learn more) — still not bait |
| Emojis / tone       | Slightly more latitude                                                            | More conservative; keep casual tone and emoji use restrained               |

Posting page-style content (announcement, third-person "we") on a personal profile
wastes the profile's credibility; posting personal-style content (first-person story)
on a company page usually reads oddly for a brand. Because pages can't rely on a
social graph the way individuals do, company-page copy should also be self-contained
and written so that employees can reshare it in their own voice without needing to
add much of their own framing.

## Quality Checklist

Before presenting output, verify:

- [ ] Hook is tight, unbroken prose within ~140 characters, no blank line before the fold
- [ ] Character count matches the target for the account type (1,300–2,500 personal /
      ≤700–800 company page)
- [ ] At least 14 short paragraphs (personal-profile posts); sentences average
      10–19 words, trending toward the shorter end
- [ ] 0–3 emojis, used structurally, not decoratively (skip if brand voice prohibits)
- [ ] CTA is specific, singular, easy to answer, and invites substantive reply — not a
      binary reaction or engagement-bait phrase
- [ ] Hashtags, if used, are 3–5 relevant tags at the end (omitting is a fine default)
- [ ] If a link is included, it's in the post body where it adds real value — not
      hidden in the first comment as a reach workaround
- [ ] No trailing em-dashes; no rhetorical antitheses used as stylistic devices; no
      AI-slop clichés; no uniform sentence rhythm
- [ ] Every substantive claim has a specific anchor (number, name, concrete outcome)
- [ ] Voice and length are calibrated to personal-profile or company-page use, per
      the table above
- [ ] Tone is consistent with brand voice (if brand-voice.md is present)
- [ ] No exclamation marks (or max 1 in launch posts)
